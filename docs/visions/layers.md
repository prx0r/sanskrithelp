### Why this is especially good for Hindi

Hindi inherits almost the entire articulatory skeleton.

Your distinction:

```text
ka kha ga gha ṅa
```

is still directly valuable.

So are:

```text
ṭa ṭha ḍa ḍha ṇa
vs
ta tha da dha na
```

That retroflex/dental distinction is one of the exact things an English speaker needs to learn properly for Hindi.

Likewise aspiration:

```text
क / ख
ग / घ

त / थ
द / ध
```

So installing the Sanskrit phonological matrix now directly trains your Hindi ear and mouth.

But Hindi then gets an **extension ring**, not a replacement Mātṛkā:

```text
क़  q
ख़  x
ग़  ɣ
ज़  z
फ़  f
ड़  ɽ
ढ़  ɽʱ
```

Those are historically later Indo-Persian/modern Hindi phonemes and **should not receive invented Abhinavagupta body loci**.

They can inherit only their **articulatory coordinate**.

For example:

```text
फ़
f

Trika nyāsa locus: NONE
```

That's epistemically clean.

### There are also Sanskrit sounds Hindi doesn't use identically

This is useful because the system can explicitly show divergence.

For example classical Sanskrit:

`ṛ` = syllabic /r̩/

whereas modern Hindi ऋ is ordinarily pronounced much closer to **ri** in Sanskrit-derived words.

Similarly Sanskrit distinctions like:

`ś / ṣ / s`

are phonologically important in Sanskrit, while actual Hindi pronunciation can merge or modify some of those distinctions depending on word/register/speaker.

And Sanskrit's:

`ḷ / ḹ`

are essentially irrelevant to ordinary Hindi conversation.

So you install the **full Sanskrit system because Sanskrit is your root language**, and Hindi simply projects its living phonology onto it.

That is far better than designing the alphabet around Hindi.
